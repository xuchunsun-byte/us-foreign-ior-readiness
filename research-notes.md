### 1.4 19 U.S.C. 1484 (who may file / who is “importer of record”)

- United States Code, 2023 Edition, Title 19, section 1484, from GovInfo: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title19/html/USCODE-2023-title19-chap4-subtitleIII-partIII-sec1484.htm
- Retrieved: 2026-11-13
- Local copy: `/workspace/us-ior-readiness/sources/19usc1484.txt`

Key definitional sentence (19 U.S.C. 1484(a)(2)(B)):

> “When an entry of merchandise is made under this section, the required documentation or information shall be filed or electronically transmitted either by the owner or purchaser of the merchandise or, when appropriately designated by the owner, purchaser, or consignee of the merchandise, a person holding a valid license under section 1641 of this title. When a consignee declares on entry that he is the owner or purchaser of merchandise the Customs Service may, without liability, accept the declaration. For the purposes of this chapter, the importer of record must be one of the parties who is eligible to file the documentation or information required by this section.”

19 U.S.C. 1484(a)(1) also states that, except as provided in sections 1490, 1498, 1552, and 1553, “one of the parties qualifying as ‘importer of record’ under paragraph (2)(B), either in person or by an agent authorized by the party in writing, shall, using reasonable care—” make and complete entry.

The IFR’s new terms **“U.S. importer of record”** and **“foreign importer of record”** are **not** in 19 U.S.C. 1484. They are created in new 19 CFR part 110. Being an IOR under the statute is a separate question from whether that IOR qualifies as a “U.S. IOR” under § 110.2.

### 1.5 Executive Order 14411 (policy direction; not the regulation)

- Title: Strengthening Customs Enforcement
- Signed: June 3, 2026
- Published: 91 FR 35125, June 10, 2026; FR Doc. 2026-11595
- URL: https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/full_text/html/2026/06/10/2026-11595.html (official text also retrieved via the Federal Register full-text endpoint)
- Retrieved: 2026-11-13

The IFR states that it is “consistent with Section 2” of this order. Section 2(c)(i) directs, for formal entry, that a foreign IOR “(2) be validated in CBP’s Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT), if determined by CBP to be eligible, or use a CTPAT validated and licensed customs broker to file entries with CBP.”

**Do not treat the EO as if it were the IFR.** In particular:

- EO Sec. 2(b)(i) directs the Secretary to prohibit a foreign IOR from filing informal entry. The **IFR regulatory text does not enact a blanket informal-entry ban.** New 19 CFR 141.21 allows specified informal consumption entries (those required to be filed on CBP Form 3461 or 7501, or electronic equivalents) if the foreign IOR is CTPAT-validated (and has the right to make entry) or designates a CTPAT-validated broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641. The IFR preamble also invited comment on a possible alternative of prohibiting informal entries by foreign IORs — that alternative is **not** the adopted regulatory text.
- EO Sec. 2(c)(i)(1) addresses continuous-bond use by foreign IORs. That continuous-bond restriction was **not found** in the IFR’s amendatory text for parts 110, 111, 141, 142, and 171 as published at 91 FR 69977. It is **not** included as a checklist item.

## 2. Key official definitions (quote these; do not substitute unofficial labels)

### 2.1 “U.S. importer of record” — 19 CFR 110.2(a) (added by the IFR)

Official regulatory text (91 FR 70005):

> “§ 110.2  U.S. importer of record.  
> (a) Qualification as U.S. importer of record. To be considered a U.S. importer of record, a party must be:  
> (1) An individual who is a United States citizen or lawful permanent resident; or  
> (2) An entity that meets the following requirements:  
> (i) The entity must be located in the United States;  
> (ii) The entity must be organized under the laws of the United States; and  
> (iii) Either:  
> (A) At all times, at least 51% of beneficial owner(s) of the entity, as defined in § 110.1, must be United States citizens or lawful permanent residents and have control of the entity; or  
> (B) The entity must maintain direct ownership of a significant amount of unencumbered real property in the United States at all times.”

Preamble clarification on encumbrance (91 FR 69982), close paraphrase of official text: the property “can be subject to some encumberment, but the net unencumbered value of the real property must be of a significant amount.”

**Wording note:** The official word is **“significant,”** not “substantial.” The checklist and landing page use “significant.” The rule does **not** quantify a dollar threshold for “significant amount.”

**Organization under U.S. law is not, by itself, enough.** Section 110.2(a)(2) is conjunctive: location in the United States, organization under U.S. law, **and** either the 51% beneficial-ownership-with-control test **or** the unencumbered-real-property test. The product may say this as a comparison of the three published criteria. It must not invent a separate “mere registration is forbidden” rule beyond those criteria.

**“Located in the United States” is subject to CBP verification.** Section 110.2(b) states that qualification “is subject to verification by CBP” and “will vary depending upon the circumstances in each instance.” CBP “will take into account, among other considerations, the size and scale of the overall operations of the entity and whether it is an instrumentality of a foreign manufacturer without a substantial presence in the United States,” and may consider a non-exhaustive list of criteria (principal place of business; physical presence where substantial business transactions are conducted; sufficient unencumbered physical domestic assets; significant unencumbered real property; whether gross sales generally exceed cost of goods sold and the entity is anticipated to generate its own profit; whether the entity is sufficiently capitalized).

**Puerto Rico:** The CBP Reference Guide (retrieved 2026-11-13) says an entity must be “located in the United States (including Puerto Rico).” The words “including Puerto Rico” do **not** appear in the § 110.2(a) regulatory text. The checklist follows § 110.2 and notes the guide’s phrasing as CBP guidance only.

### 2.2 “Foreign importer of record” — 19 CFR 110.3

> “An importer of record who does not meet the criteria to be considered a U.S. importer of record pursuant to § 110.2 is a foreign importer of record.”

There is no separate nationality test in § 110.3. A U.S.-organized company can still be a foreign IOR if it fails § 110.2(a)(2). A non-U.S. company that is not a U.S. citizen or LPR individual will not meet § 110.2(a) and is a foreign IOR under this definition.

### 2.3 “Beneficial owner” — 19 CFR 110.1 (abbreviated)

A beneficial owner is a natural person who, directly or indirectly, “Exercises substantial control over the entity; or Owns or controls not less than 25 percent of the ownership interests of the entity.” The definition “Includes circumstances where multiple layers of entity ownership exist” (look-through). Every entity “must have at least one beneficial owner.”

This matters because the 51% test in § 110.2(a)(2)(iii)(A) is 51% of **beneficial owner(s) as defined in § 110.1**, who also “have control of the entity.”

### 2.4 “Physical domestic assets” — 19 CFR 110.1

> “The term ‘physical domestic assets’ means cash deposited in a U.S. bank account, U.S. Treasury bonds, and the following assets when physically located in the United States: real property, inventory, machinery, and/or equipment.”

This term is used in the **verification** criteria in § 110.2(b)(3). It is not itself a standalone qualification path in § 110.2(a). Do not treat “having some U.S. inventory or a U.S. bank account” as sufficient to be a U.S. IOR.

## 3. Official entry rules for a foreign IOR — 19 CFR 141.21

Official regulatory text (91 FR 70006):

> “(a) A foreign importer of record, as defined in § 110.3 of this chapter, may not enter merchandise for consumption except pursuant to the requirements in paragraph (b) of this section. This restriction is applicable to merchandise entered for consumption (§ 141.0a(f)) for which a formal entry is required to be filed or, if an informal entry is filed, for which an informal entry is required to be filed on CBP Form 3461 or CBP Form 7501, or their electronic equivalents.”

> “(b)(1) If the foreign importer of record is validated through the Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT) program (6 U.S.C. 961(a)), and has the right to make entry pursuant to § 143.26 of this chapter for informal entry or 19 U.S.C. 1484(a)(2)(B) for formal entry, the foreign importer of record may enter merchandise for consumption or hire a customs broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641 to make entry.”

> “(b)(2) If the foreign importer of record is not validated through the CTPAT program, the foreign importer of record must designate a customs broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641 who is validated through the CTPAT program (6 U.S.C. 961(a)) to make entry or act as the importer of record for the entry. If a customs broker acts as the importer of record for the entry on behalf of a principal on the power of attorney (see § 141.46 of this chapter) who is not an importer of record, and that principal would qualify as a foreign importer of record pursuant to § 110.3 of this chapter, the broker must also be validated through the CTPAT program. The designated customs broker who is validated through the CTPAT program must act as the importer of record for the entry if the principal on the power of attorney (§ 141.46) is not the owner or purchaser of the merchandise”

**Preamble, important qualification (91 FR 69984):** a CTPAT-validated foreign IOR “choosing to hire a customs broker may elect to hire a CTPAT validated broker, or a non-CTPAT validated broker.” Therefore the product must **not** say that every foreign IOR must use a CTPAT-validated broker. That broker requirement applies when the foreign IOR is **not** itself CTPAT-validated (and in the additional broker-as-IOR situation described in § 141.21(b)(2)).

**Official term is “validated,” not “certified.”** The landing page and PDF use “CTPAT-validated.”

**Scope is not “all imports.”** The restriction in § 141.21(a) is for merchandise entered **for consumption** that requires a **formal** entry, or an **informal** entry required to be filed on CBP Form 3461 or 7501 (or electronic equivalents). Footnote 23 in the preamble says, operationally, this applies to informal entry types 11, 12, and 13, and all formal entry types, and points to CATAIR. That footnote is preamble/operational guidance, not the regulatory sentence itself.

**Power of attorney:** revised 19 CFR 141.46: “Before transacting customs business on behalf of a principal, to include acting as an importer of record on behalf of the principal, a customs broker is required to obtain a valid power of attorney to do so.”

### 3.1 Current CTPAT importer eligibility (program rules, not the IFR text)

The IFR does not itself say that only Canadian companies may become CTPAT-validated foreign IORs. Two official CBP sources, however, currently limit **importer** CTPAT eligibility in a way that matters to UK/EU/AU sellers:

1. CBP IFR Overview PDF (Pub. 5753-1126, 11/06/2026): “Be validated participants in the Customs Trade Partnership Against Terrorism (CTPAT) program **(Note: currently, only Canadian foreign IORs are eligible for CTPAT validation)**; or Designate a CTPAT validated licensed customs broker to make entry.”
2. CBP CTPAT Importers page (retrieved 2026-11-13): first eligibility requirement is “Be an active U.S. Importer or Non-Resident Canadian Importer. Active is defined as having imported goods into the U.S. within the past 12 months.” Other published requirements include an active U.S. IOR number, a valid continuous import bond, and a business office staffed in the United States or Canada.

The checklist may **point** sellers to these official pages and may note CBP’s own “currently, only Canadian foreign IORs” statement as **CBP guidance as of the retrieval date**, which can change. The product must **not** write a CTPAT application, must not promise eligibility or validation, and must not treat the Canada limitation as if it were a sentence in 19 CFR 141.21.

## 4. Official rules if the party claims to be a U.S. IOR — 19 CFR 141.22 and 111.29

**Declaration (19 CFR 141.22(a)(1)):** A U.S. IOR who has the right to make entry “may enter merchandise subject to the more specific requirements and procedures set forth elsewhere in this chapter. At the time of filing an entry (§ 141.0a(a)) and entry summary (§ 141.0a(b)), the importer of record must declare its qualification as a U.S. importer of record as defined in § 110.2 of this chapter.”

**Broker verification (19 CFR 111.29(a)(2)):**

> “A broker, prior to filing a declaration on behalf of a client pursuant to § 141.22 of this chapter, must verify that the client meets the requirements to qualify as a U.S. importer of record pursuant to § 110.2 of this chapter. Each broker must request and review a client’s records to determine that the criteria to be considered a U.S. importer of record as specified in § 110.2 of this chapter, are satisfied. Such records may include, but are not limited to, proof of citizenship or lawful permanent residence, corporate charters, financial statements and records, purchase receipts, or documents of title.”

**Five-year retention (19 CFR 111.23(b)):**

> “Records related to a verification conducted pursuant to § 111.29(a)(2) must be retained for five years from the date the verification is conducted.”

This five-year duty is imposed on **the broker**. The checklist may tell a seller to expect a records request and to avoid claiming U.S. IOR status they do not meet. It is not a seller-facing filing form.

**Delayed compliance is broker-side, and only for § 111.29 verification/recordkeeping.** Section VII (91 FR 69986): CBP “will accept requests from brokers to delay compliance with the new verification requirement for customs brokers in § 111.29.” Requests “must be submitted by January 5, 2027.” “All customs brokers are expected to be in full compliance with the requirements of this rule no later than May 6, 2027.” CBP’s Delayed Compliance page (retrieved 2026-11-13) states that brokers who submit a valid, complete, timely request, receive an acknowledged receipt, and do not receive a notice of ineligibility “will be granted a delayed compliance period until May 6, 2027, to be in full compliance with the U.S. IOR verification and record keeping requirements as set forth in 19 CFR § 111,” and “No additional extensions will be granted beyond this date.” Ineligible categories (e.g., CTPAT removal, 5106 privilege revocation, SAM.gov exclusion, filer-code suspension, license revocation proceedings) are set out in Section VII and on that CBP page.

## 5. DDP (Delivered Duty Paid)

The IFR, 19 U.S.C. 1484, and the CBP guidance documents retrieved for this project **do not define DDP or state that a DDP seller is automatically the importer of record.** DDP is a commercial Incoterms allocation of costs and formalities. Who may be the IOR remains a question of 19 U.S.C. 1484(a)(2)(B) and the entry documents. The checklist may ask a DDP seller to compare their commercial contract with who will actually be named as IOR, and must not say “DDP means you are the IOR under U.S. law.”

## 6. Checklist items included in the sample PDF / landing page, with citations

Each item is a comparison check or an official next step, not legal advice.

1. **Identify who will be the importer of record on the U.S. entry** — 19 U.S.C. 1484(a)(1) and (a)(2)(B). IOR must be the owner or purchaser, or a person holding a valid license under 19 U.S.C. 1641 when appropriately designated by the owner, purchaser, or consignee.
2. **Do not treat a DDP clause as a legal IOR designation** — no official CBP/IFR definition of DDP; compare the commercial arrangement with the actual entry (item 1).
3. **If a broker will transact customs business or act as IOR, confirm a valid power of attorney** — 19 CFR 141.46 (as revised by the IFR).
4. **Test whether the IOR qualifies as a “U.S. importer of record”** — 19 CFR 110.2(a)(1)–(2), using the official “significant amount” wording and the conjunctive entity tests. Include the beneficial-owner definition in 19 CFR 110.1 when applying the 51% test.
5. **Treat qualification as subject to CBP verification; “located in the United States” is facts-and-circumstances** — 19 CFR 110.2(b). Do not treat a U.S. bank account or inventory alone as sufficient (physical domestic assets are a verification criterion, not a § 110.2(a) path).
6. **If the IOR does not meet § 110.2, the IOR is a “foreign importer of record”** — 19 CFR 110.3.
7. **Confirm the entry is in the § 141.21(a) scope** (consumption; formal entry, or informal entry required on Form 3461 or 7501 or electronic equivalents) before applying the foreign-IOR entry restriction.
8. **If the party is a foreign IOR and is CTPAT-validated and has the right to make entry:** may enter, or hire a broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641 (the hired broker need not be CTPAT-validated) — 19 CFR 141.21(b)(1) and IFR preamble at 91 FR 69984.
9. **If the party is a foreign IOR and is not CTPAT-validated:** must designate a customs broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641 who is CTPAT-validated, to make entry or act as IOR — 19 CFR 141.21(b)(2).
10. **If the principal on the power of attorney is not the owner or purchaser, the designated CTPAT-validated broker must act as IOR** — 19 CFR 141.21(b)(2).
11. **Check current official CTPAT importer eligibility before assuming a non-Canadian foreign IOR can become CTPAT-validated** — CBP IFR Overview note and CBP CTPAT Importers eligibility page (guidance / program rules; not IFR text). Point to official pages only; do not prepare an application.
12. **Confirm broker license (19 U.S.C. 1641) and, where item 9 applies, CTPAT-validated status on CBP’s official list** — 19 CFR 141.21(b)(2); CBP list URL on the Strengthening Customs Enforcement page. No named broker is recommended.
13. **If U.S. IOR status will be declared, the declaration is made at entry and entry summary, and the broker must verify first and keep verification records for five years from the verification date** — 19 CFR 141.22(a)(1), 111.29(a)(2), 111.23(b).
14. **Calendar check, without collapsing dates:** IFR effective 5 January 2027; comments due 5 January 2027; broker delayed-compliance requests for § 111.29 due 5 January 2027; full broker compliance no later than 6 May 2027 — 91 FR 69977 and Section VII; CBP Delayed Compliance page.
15. **Official next steps only:** read the IFR (Federal Register / GovInfo PDF); review CBP hub, overview, and reference guide; comment on docket USCBP-2026-1387 by 5 January 2027; consult the seller’s own licensed customs broker or qualified counsel (unnamed).

## 7. Items refused or limited because the official text does not support them

- Any statement that **every** foreign IOR must use a CTPAT-validated broker (false if the foreign IOR is itself CTPAT-validated under § 141.21(b)(1)).
- Using “CTPAT-certified” as if it were the official term (the IFR says “validated through the CTPAT program”).
- A dollar threshold for “significant amount” of unencumbered real property, or any other numeric asset floor not in the IFR text.
- “Substantial amount” of real property (supervisor paraphrase; official word is “significant”).
- “DDP means you are the IOR under U.S. law.”
- A blanket ban on informal entry by foreign IORs (EO 14411 Sec. 2(b) language; **not** the adopted IFR text).
- A continuous-bond prohibition for foreign IORs (EO 14411 Sec. 2(c)(i)(1); **not found** in the IFR amendatory text reviewed).
- Treating “including Puerto Rico” as if it were in § 110.2(a) (it appears in the CBP Reference Guide only).
- Instructions for completing a CTPAT application, or a promise of CTPAT validation or cargo clearance.
- Recommendation of a specific customs broker, or any “CBP-approved” / official-seal language.
- Collapsing the 5 January 2027 entry effective date with the 6 May 2027 broker delayed-compliance end date.
- Mixing in the separate Low-Value Shipment NPRM or other EO 14411 workstreams as if they were this IFR.
- A live price, checkout, payment URL, or buy button on the preview.

## 8. Residual uncertainties / revision risk (disclose on the product)

- This is an **Interim Final Rule**. Comments are due 5 January 2027. CBP’s 5 November 2026 media release states comments “will be considered in determining whether to amend, confirm, or withdraw the rule following its interim implementation.” Details may change.
- New 19 CFR part 110 is added by this IFR and is **effective 5 January 2027**. Until then, eCFR may not yet display part 110. Cite the IFR text, not a premature eCFR snapshot, unless eCFR has been updated.
- “Significant amount,” “located in the United States,” and several § 110.2(b) verification criteria are qualitative and reserved to CBP’s case-by-case determination.
- CTPAT **importer** eligibility is a separate program and can change; CBP’s “currently, only Canadian foreign IORs” note is guidance as of 6 November 2026 / retrieval on 13 November 2026.
- Informal-entry operational scope (preamble footnote 23: types 11, 12, and 13) may need confirmation against current CATAIR and CBP systems guidance.
- Broker delayed compliance, if granted, does not, on the face of Section VII, postpone the 5 January 2027 effective date of the foreign-IOR entry restriction in § 141.21.
