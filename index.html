Who it is for

Written for a non-expert who needs to see which official questions are still open — not for filing an entry, and not as a substitute for the people who file entries.

Non-U.S. sellers shipping DDP to the United States

Priority readers are UK and EU brands and suppliers. The same official tests also matter to sellers in Canada, Australia, and other English-speaking markets. Delivered Duty Paid is a commercial term; it does not, by itself, legally name the Importer of Record.

Sellers who act as Importer of Record

If you (or a broker acting for you) will be named on a U.S. consumption entry, the Interim Final Rule distinguishes a “U.S. importer of record” from a “foreign importer of record.” Those labels are defined in the rule, not in everyday contract language.

U.S. buyers checking a vendor’s arrangements

A secondary use is to see whether a vendor’s current broker and IOR plan still matches the official tests. This checklist does not vet or recommend any broker.

What the snapshot checks

Each item is a comparison against official text, with a next official step. The sample PDF is designed to be worked through in about ten minutes.

A. Who is the Importer of Record?

Under 19 U.S.C. 1484, the importer of record must be the owner or purchaser of the merchandise, or a person holding a valid license under 19 U.S.C. 1641 who has been appropriately designated by the owner, purchaser, or consignee. A DDP clause is not a substitute for that statutory test or for the name on the entry.

B. Do you qualify as a “U.S. IOR”?

New 19 CFR 110.2 defines a “U.S. importer of record.” An individual must be a U.S. citizen or lawful permanent resident. An entity must be located in the United States, organized under U.S. law, and at all times either have at least 51% beneficial owners who are U.S. citizens or lawful permanent residents and who have control, or directly own a significant amount of unencumbered U.S. real property. Anyone who does not meet that test is a “foreign importer of record” under 19 CFR 110.3. Qualification is subject to CBP verification.

C. If you are a foreign IOR, how may entry be made?

From 5 January 2027, for consumption entries in the rule’s scope, a foreign IOR may enter in its own name only if it is CTPAT-validated and has the right to make entry. A foreign IOR who is already CTPAT-validated and has the right to make entry may hire a customs broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641; that hired broker need not be CTPAT-validated. If the foreign IOR is not CTPAT-validated, it must designate a customs broker licensed under 19 U.S.C. 1641 who is also CTPAT-validated. If the principal on the power of attorney is not the owner or purchaser, that designated broker must act as Importer of Record. The official term is “validated,” not “certified.”

D. Which official step is still missing?

The checklist points to official sources: the Federal Register rule, CBP pages, the CTPAT importer eligibility page, and CBP’s list of CTPAT-validated brokers. It does not complete a CTPAT application, name a broker, or tell you that clearance will occur.

Two 2027 dates that should not be collapsed

5 January 2027 The Interim Final Rule “is effective on January 5, 2027.” Public comments must be received on or before this date. Licensed customs brokers who wish to request delayed compliance with the changes in 19 CFR 111.29 must do so by this date. For goods and foreign-IOR entry restrictions, this is the effective date stated in the rule.
6 May 2027 The rule states that all customs brokers are expected to be in full compliance no later than May 6, 2027. That later date relates to delayed compliance with the broker verification requirements in 19 CFR 111.29. It does not, on the face of the official text, postpone the 5 January 2027 effective date for foreign-IOR entry restrictions.

Source: DATES paragraph and Section VII of the Interim Final Rule, 91 FR 69977. CBP’s Delayed Compliance page describes the broker request process.
